How logistics works

Nine links. Break one and the customer feels it.

People picture logistics as trucks. Trucks are one link. What actually determines whether a shipment lands on the promised day is the chain around them — and every link has its own failure mode.

01

Demand & procurement

Someone forecasts what will sell, then buys it. Purchase orders go out with a lead time attached — the gap between "we ordered it" and "it can physically be here." Get the forecast wrong and every downstream link is either starved or drowning.

02

Inbound freight

Goods travel from the supplier to your building — often by ocean container, then drayage from the port, then a domestic leg. This is the longest and least controllable stretch, which is why inbound is where buffers get built.

DrayageContainerCustoms
03

Receiving & put-away

The trailer backs in and the count begins. Received quantities are checked against the advance ship notice, damage is recorded before it becomes an argument, and each pallet gets a home. A shipment that isn't received correctly is invisible inventory — it exists, but nothing can sell it.

ASNCycle countPut-away
04

Inventory, slotting & safety stock

Fast-moving items sit at waist height near the pack line; slow ones go up and back. Safety stock covers the variance between forecast and reality. Slotting is the single cheapest lever in a warehouse: it costs a weekend of relabelling and pays back in every pick, forever.

ABC velocitySafety stockSlotting
05

Order management & wave planning

Orders are allocated against real stock and released to the floor in waves grouped by carrier cut-off, service level and pick path. Releasing everything at once looks efficient and isn't — it just moves the queue from the screen to the floor.

AllocationWavesCut-off
06

Pick, pack & manifest

A picker walks a route designed to minimise steps, not to match the order on the screen. Packing chooses the smallest box that protects the goods, because you pay to ship air. The label is the moment the shipment enters the carrier's system and becomes trackable.

07

Mode selection & line haul

Parcel, LTL, full truckload, intermodal or air — chosen by weight, cube, distance and how badly the date matters. The right mode is rarely the cheapest one on the rate sheet; it's the cheapest one that still hits the promised delivery date.

Cross-dock & last mile

Regional hubs break long-haul trailers down and rebuild them into local routes. Then the final leg: the shortest distance and the highest cost per mile, decided almost entirely by how many stops fit on one run.

Reverse logistics

A percentage is coming back — that is not a failure, it is a line item. Returns need their own lane: inspect, restock, refurbish or scrap. Businesses that treat returns as an afterthought lose the margin they made on the outbound leg.

The four levers

What actually moves your freight bill.

Density, not weight

Carriers price the space you occupy as much as the pounds you ship. A light, bulky pallet is billed at a dimensional weight far above what the scale says. Packing tighter is often a bigger saving than renegotiating a rate.

Dwell time

Every hour a trailer sits at a dock is an hour it isn't earning, and after the free window it starts billing you as detention. Fast, predictable loading is worth more to a carrier than a slightly better rate — and they price accordingly.

On-time in-full

OTIF measures the only thing a receiver cares about: did everything arrive, on the day it was promised. Partial deliveries and reschedules generate chargebacks that dwarf the freight cost you saved by cutting a corner upstream.

Empty miles

A truck that runs back empty still burns fuel and hours, and that cost is recovered from the load that went out. Matching a backhaul to your outbound lane is the difference between a rate that holds and one that creeps up every quarter.
